Co-Parenting Schedules: 7 Custody Calendar Patterns Explained

By Reid, a parent and the maker of FamSync. October 20, 2026.

If you're working out a parenting plan, you've probably seen a string of numbers like "2-2-3" or "2-2-5-5" and wondered what they actually look like on a calendar. This guide walks through the seven most common patterns, what a typical two weeks looks like for each, and which families they tend to suit.

Important: this is a plain-language explainer, not legal advice. Custody arrangements depend on your state, your agreement or court order, and your kids. Talk to a family law attorney or mediator about your specific situation.

(Disclosure: I make FamSync, a family organizer app with a shared calendar. It comes up once near the end.)

How to read the numbers

The numbers describe how many days in a row the kids spend with each parent before switching. "2-2-3" means 2 days with Parent A, 2 days with Parent B, 3 days with Parent A, and then the next week it flips.

In the examples below, A and B are the two homes. Each line is one week, Monday to Sunday.

1. Alternating weeks (7-7)

Week 1:  A A A A A A A
Week 2:  B B B B B B B

The kids spend a full week in one home, then a full week in the other. Handoffs often happen on Friday after school or Sunday evening.

Pros: Only one handoff a week. Simple to remember. Kids settle in to each home.
Cons: Seven days is a long time away from a parent, especially for younger children.
Often suits: School-age kids and teens, parents who live a bit further apart, families where handoffs are tense (fewer of them).

A common variation adds a midweek dinner with the other parent, so no one goes a full week without seeing the kids.

2. 2-2-3

Week 1:  A A B B A A A
Week 2:  B B A A B B B

Two days with A, two with B, then a three-day weekend with A. The next week it flips, so each parent gets alternating long weekends.

Pros: Kids never go more than three days without seeing each parent. Weekends alternate fairly.
Cons: Lots of handoffs (three a week). The pattern shifts every week, which can be hard to track.
Often suits: Younger children who do better with shorter stretches. Parents who live close to each other and the school.

3. 2-2-5-5 (also called 5-2-2-5)

Week 1:  A A B B A A A
Week 2:  A A B B B B B

Parent A always has Monday and Tuesday. Parent B always has Wednesday and Thursday. The weekends (Friday to Sunday) alternate. Combined, each parent gets a five-day stretch.

Pros: The weekdays are fixed, which makes activities, carpools and work schedules much easier. "Tuesdays are always at Dad's" is easy for kids to remember.
Cons: Five-day stretches can feel long for very young kids.
Often suits: School-age kids with regular weekday activities. Parents with predictable work weeks.

4. 3-4-4-3

Week 1:  A A A B B B B
Week 2:  A A A A B B B

In week 1, Parent A has three days and Parent B has four. In week 2 it flips: A has four, B has three. (Exact starting days vary by family.)

Pros: Fewer handoffs than 2-2-3, shorter stretches than alternating weeks. Exactly 50/50 over two weeks, and most weekdays stay with the same parent.
Cons: One weekday (Thursday, in the example above) swaps homes every week, so an activity on that day lands with a different parent each time.
Often suits: Families who want 50/50 with a middle ground on stretch length.

5. Every other weekend (plus a midweek visit)

Week 1:  A A A A B B B
Week 2:  A A B A A A A     (B has a midweek evening or overnight)

The kids live primarily with Parent A. Parent B has every other weekend, often Friday to Sunday, and frequently a midweek dinner or overnight.

Pros: One stable home base during the school week. Clear and traditional, so schools and family understand it.
Cons: Parent B has much less everyday time, which can be hard on the relationship.
Often suits: Parents who live far apart, very different work schedules, or situations where one home is clearly better placed for school.

6. Weekdays and weekends (5-2)

Every week:  A A A A A B B

One parent has the school week. The other has every weekend.

Pros: Simple and fixed. Each home has a clear role.
Cons: One parent does all the school-morning work and never gets a relaxed weekend. The other rarely sees the homework-and-bedtime side of life. Many families eventually alternate one weekend a month so it isn't so lopsided.
Often suits: Parents whose work schedules split naturally (for example, one works weekends).

7. Extended weekends (60/40 or 70/30 variations)

Week 1:  A A A A B B B     (B has Thu evening to Mon morning)
Week 2:  A A A A A A A

Parent B's alternating weekends run longer, often Thursday after school to Monday drop-off at school. This gives the "weekend parent" more meaningful time, including school-day mornings.

Pros: More time and more school involvement for Parent B, without a full 50/50 split.
Cons: Requires both homes to be reasonably close to school.
Often suits: Families moving from every-other-weekend toward more balance, or where a full 50/50 isn't practical.

Holidays, birthdays and school breaks

The regular rotation is only half the plan. Holidays are where most conflict happens, so many parenting plans layer a holiday schedule on top that overrides the normal pattern. Common approaches:

  • Alternate by year. Parent A has Thanksgiving in odd years, Parent B in even years.
  • Split the holiday. Thanksgiving morning and lunch with one parent, evening with the other. Works when homes are close.
  • Split the break. Winter break divided in half, with the halves alternating each year (so the same parent doesn't always have Christmas Day).
  • Fixed holidays. Some families assign holidays that matter to one side (Mother's Day with Mom, a cultural or religious holiday with the parent who observes it) every year.
  • Kids' birthdays. Some families alternate; some give each parent a few hours on the day; some celebrate twice.

With Thanksgiving and winter break coming up, it's worth doing one thing now: put the holiday plan on the calendar for the rest of the year, explicitly, even if it's already in your agreement. "Week of Dec 22: with Dad. Dec 29 to Jan 4: with Mom." It prevents the classic December misunderstanding.

Choosing a schedule: questions to ask

  • How far apart are the homes? Frequent-handoff schedules (2-2-3) need homes close to each other and the school.
  • How old are the kids? Younger kids often do better with shorter stretches. Teens often want fewer handoffs and a say in the plan.
  • What do the weekdays look like? If a kid has fixed weekday activities, fixed-weekday schedules (2-2-5-5) make life easier.
  • How do handoffs go? If they're tense, fewer is better. Handoffs at school (one parent drops off, the other picks up) can remove face-to-face friction.
  • What do the work schedules allow? A plan that looks fair on paper but doesn't fit anyone's job won't last.

Making any schedule work day to day

The schedule is the skeleton. What makes it work is the everyday logistics, and that's where most friction comes from: the cleats left at the other house, the field trip form nobody signed, the dentist appointment booked on the wrong parent's day.

A few habits that help across every pattern:

  1. One shared calendar for the kids, visible to both homes. Not two calendars that each hold half the information. Both parents see the same practices, appointments and handoffs.
  2. Mark the custody days themselves on the calendar. Seeing whose day it is at a glance prevents a lot of "I thought you had them."
  3. Put the kids' stuff in the event notes. "Bring the science project." "Inhaler is in the front pocket."
  4. Let older kids see the calendar too. Kids who can see where they're sleeping on Thursday feel more in control.
  5. Agree on how changes get made. Swaps happen. Decide in advance that a swap isn't real until it's on the shared calendar.

The short version

  • 7-7: one handoff a week, long stretches. Good for older kids.
  • 2-2-3: short stretches, many handoffs. Good for little kids and close homes.
  • 2-2-5-5: fixed weekdays, alternating weekends. Great for activities.
  • 3-4-4-3: a middle ground, exactly 50/50 over two weeks.
  • Every other weekend + midweek: one home base.
  • 5-2: weekdays at one home, weekends at the other.
  • Extended weekends: more balance without a full 50/50.

Layer a written holiday plan on top, put everything on one shared calendar, and revisit the plan as your kids grow. What works for a 4-year-old rarely works for a 14-year-old.
