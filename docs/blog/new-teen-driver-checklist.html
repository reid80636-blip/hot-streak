Your Teen Just Got Their License: A Calm Parent's Checklist

By Reid, a parent and the maker of FamSync. October 20, 2026.

The first time your teenager backs out of the driveway alone is a very particular feeling. Pride, mostly. And then, about ninety seconds later, a strong urge to text them, which you absolutely should not do, because they're driving.

The worry isn't irrational. According to the CDC, teen drivers ages 16 to 19 have a fatal crash rate almost three times that of drivers 20 and older, per mile driven, and crash risk is particularly high during the first months of having a license. (CDC, Risk Factors for Teen Drivers)

The good news is that the risks are well understood, and most of what reduces them is stuff parents can actually do. This checklist covers the first month or so, in roughly the order it tends to come up.

(Disclosure: I make FamSync, a family organizer app that includes a family map and drive summaries. I'll mention it once, in the check-in section. Everything else here is tool-agnostic.)

1. Know your state's license rules (they're stricter than you think)

Every US state has some form of graduated driver licensing (GDL): a learner stage, then an intermediate license with restrictions, then a full license. The intermediate stage usually limits:

  • Nighttime driving (often a curfew on unsupervised driving after a certain hour)
  • Passengers (often a limit on how many non-family teens can ride along)
  • Phone use (many states ban any phone use for new drivers, even hands-free)

The rules vary a lot by state, and many parents don't know the specifics. IIHS keeps a state-by-state table of graduated licensing laws. Look up yours with your teen, so the rules come from the state and not from you.

2. Put your family rules in writing

State law is the floor. Your family rules can be stricter, and they work best written down and signed by everyone.

The CDC offers a free Parent-Teen Driving Agreement you can print. It covers the basics and has space for your own restrictions. The idea is that your teen earns more freedom as they show they're ready, and the agreement spells out exactly how.

Things worth agreeing on:

  • Passengers. The CDC notes that teen or young adult passengers increase the crash risk of unsupervised teen drivers, and the risk goes up with each additional passenger. A common family rule: no teen passengers for the first six months, then one, then more.
  • Night driving. The fatal crash rate at night for teen drivers is about three times that of adults ages 30 to 59, per mile (CDC). A family curfew for driving, separate from the "be home by" curfew, makes sense in the first year.
  • Seat belts, every seat, every trip. In 2020, 56% of teen drivers and passengers ages 16 to 19 killed in crashes weren't wearing a seat belt (CDC). Make it a "the car doesn't move" rule.
  • Phones. More on this below.
  • Weather. Many families add "no driving in snow or ice until you've practiced it with a parent."
  • Consequences. What happens if a rule is broken? Agree now, calmly, instead of inventing it at 11pm.
  • The "call me, no questions" rule. If your teen or the driver they're with isn't safe to drive, they call you and you come get them, with no lecture that night. This one matters more than almost anything else on the list.

Stick the agreement on the fridge and plan to revisit it in three months.

3. Sort out insurance before the first solo drive

Call your insurer before your teen drives alone, not after. Questions to ask:

  • When does my teen need to be added to the policy? (Some insurers require it at the learner stage.)
  • Which car will they be listed as the primary driver of? This can change the price a lot.
  • Are there discounts for good grades, a driver's ed course, or a telematics program?
  • What happens to our rate after a first claim?

Also decide who pays for what. Gas, insurance increase, tickets. Writing it into the agreement avoids a lot of arguments later.

4. Stock the car

A small kit in the trunk or glovebox, so your teen isn't stuck if something goes wrong:

  • Proof of insurance and registration (and know where they are)
  • A phone charging cable
  • A small first aid kit
  • A flashlight
  • Jumper cables (and a lesson on how to use them)
  • An ice scraper and a blanket if you live somewhere cold
  • A written list of emergency numbers, in case the phone dies
  • A little cash

Then do a 20-minute "car basics" session: how to check tire pressure, where the spare is, what each dashboard warning light means, how to pump gas, and exactly what to do after a minor accident (move to safety, call you, take photos, exchange information, don't argue about fault).

5. Decide the phone rules (and use the tools that exist)

The CDC notes that in 2019, 39% of high school students who drove texted or emailed while driving at least once in the prior 30 days. Distraction is especially dangerous for new drivers because they don't yet have the spare attention experienced drivers do.

Practical rules that tend to stick:

  • Phone goes in a mount or the back seat, not the lap.
  • Navigation is set before the car moves.
  • Turn on Driving Focus. On iPhone, the built-in Driving Focus can silence notifications and auto-reply to texts while driving. Set it up together so it turns on automatically.
  • Parents don't text or call a driving teen. This one is on us. If you need them, wait until they've arrived, or call and let it go to voicemail.
  • Model it. If you check your phone at red lights, they will too.

6. Keep practicing together after the license

The license is the start of learning to drive, not the end. In the first few months, keep riding along sometimes, especially for situations they haven't faced alone:

  • Highway merging at rush hour
  • Driving at night
  • Rain, and snow if you get it
  • A long trip on unfamiliar roads
  • Parking somewhere hard (a crowded mall in December)

Make it a normal thing ("you drive us to the grocery store this weekend") rather than a test.

7. Check in without hovering

This is the part most parents struggle with. You want to know they got there safely. They want to not feel watched. Both are reasonable.

A few approaches that respect both:

  • A simple arrival text. "Here" is enough. Make it part of the agreement.
  • Location sharing, discussed openly. If you use it, tell your teen, explain why, and agree on when it might be relaxed. Secret tracking tends to backfire badly once it's discovered.
  • Arrival and departure alerts for a few regular places (school, work, practice), so you get a quiet notice instead of needing to ask.
  • Talk about drives, not just destinations. How did the highway go? Anything sketchy happen?

For families who want more than a text, this is what I built FamSync's family map for. Everyone can see where the family is with ETAs like "home in 8 min", and you can set alerts for when someone arrives at or leaves a place like school or work. Each drive also gets a short summary when the car parks: the route, distance, top speed, hard braking and how many times the phone was picked up while driving. Teens can see that their own drives are logged, so it's never secret. The map, place alerts and drive summaries are part of FamSync Premium, and in my view they work best when the teen knows about them and you look at the summaries together rather than using them as a gotcha. A good drive summary is something to praise, too.

Whatever you use, the goal of the first year isn't surveillance. It's building enough trust, on both sides, that you can gradually let go.

8. Plan to loosen the rules on purpose

The point of all these restrictions is that they come off. Agree up front on what earning more freedom looks like. For example:

  • After 3 months with no incidents: one teen passenger allowed.
  • After 6 months: driving curfew moves later.
  • After a winter of practice: solo driving in snow allowed.

When teens can see a clear path to more independence, they're much more likely to follow the rules along the way.

The checklist, one page

  • Look up your state's GDL rules together
  • Sign a written parent-teen driving agreement
  • Agree on passengers, night driving, seat belts, phones, weather and consequences
  • Agree on the "call me, no questions" rule
  • Call the insurer and add your teen
  • Decide who pays for gas, insurance and tickets
  • Stock the car kit
  • Do a car-basics session (tires, lights, gas, accidents)
  • Turn on Driving Focus together
  • Keep practicing hard situations together
  • Agree on how you'll check in, openly
  • Set dates to review and loosen the rules

Holiday season is a good moment to go over this list again. Breaks mean more night driving, more friends in the car and, in a lot of the country, the first ice of the year.
